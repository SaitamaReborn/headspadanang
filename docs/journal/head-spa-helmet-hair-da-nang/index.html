Six hours under a rented helmet: the scooter scalp, and the Da Nang wash that answers it

Whether a head spa fixes the greasy, itchy scalp that arrives on day three of riding a Da Nang scooter · what the sebum research says about heat under a shell, why the second wash is the one that gets cut, the tier worth buying twice, and the request that keeps the mask off your roots.

In short
  • A helmet is a greenhouse, and sebum answers to temperature. A British Journal of Dermatology study on local heating of forehead skin put the change at roughly ten percent of the surface sebum rate per degree, with the authors noting that a surface reading is not the same as gland output. A randomised crossover trial in Environmental Research in 2025 found secretion significantly up after sixty and a hundred and twenty minutes at 32 degrees. Da Nang hands you 30-degree afternoons at 75 to 85 percent humidity, then you strap a padded lid over the lot.
  • The grease is the food supply for the thing that itches. A comprehensive review of seborrhoeic dermatitis and dandruff puts the two together at about half the adult population and names sebaceous secretion, fungal colonisation of the skin surface and individual susceptibility as interacting causes. The yeast at the centre of that literature, Malassezia, cannot synthesise its own fatty acids. It eats what your scalp puts out.
  • Our position here reverses what this guide tells people with dense hair: when a helmet has greased your scalp, buy the short tier, and buy it twice. The four houses that price the 25-to-30-minute wash ask 120K to 350K for it, and the pick's 25-minute wash at 120K sits at the floor of that band. Every tier removes the sebum within its first fifteen minutes. Steam, stones and a nourishing mask do not take it off.
  • Two sentences at the counter beat a tier upgrade. Gội hai lần giúp mình nhé asks for the second wash in full rather than a rinse, and đừng thoa dầu xả lên da đầu nhé keeps the conditioner and the hair mask off the scalp itself, where they do you no good whatsoever.

The city hands you a bike, then sells you the basin

Most visitors are on two wheels within a day of arriving. Along the beach streets of An Thượng and Mỹ An you pass a rental shop every forty metres, automatics go out at tourist day rates, and each ride that fills a Da Nang itinerary is a long one: the Hải Vân pass north toward Lăng Cô, the Sơn Trà peninsula loop before the heat comes, the Marble Mountains and the road south to Hội An. Helmets are required by law here for both rider and passenger. Whatever comes clipped to the handlebar therefore goes on your head, and plenty of other heads wore it before yours.

Usually the question appears on day three, in the bathroom mirror around seven in the evening. Hair at the crown has gone flat and separated into strands that stay wherever you put them. There is a shine along the parting that was not there at home. Something at the nape itches enough that you want to go at it with your nails. You washed this morning (the part that bothers people most), and then you pull up a head spa menu on your phone.

This is worth knowing before you book: the problem is not a tourist problem, and the local answer to it has been running for decades. Znews, reporting on why Chinese visitors were flocking to Vietnamese basins, relayed an explanation from the Taiwan-based travel firm Lanytrip that puts scooters at the centre of it. Riding gets hair and scalp oily fast, so a wash at a salon becomes routine rather than indulgence, at prices far under what a spa charges elsewhere. VOV reported in February 2026 that the trade had gone viral on Chinese social media, with standard hair-wash services at 50,000 to 100,000 dong.

Read that final figure with care, because it causes more sticker shock in this city than anything else. It describes the barber-sink wash: five or six minutes sitting upright at a basin. On the menus this guide covers, the reclined ritual, gội đầu dưỡng sinh, lasts 25 to 105 minutes and starts well above that; the price table gives the bands. The walkthrough sets out what the longer version includes. On whether it fixes your scalp, the honest answer is yes for the grease, partly for the itch, and not at all for the cause, which is parked on the pavement outside.

What the heat under a shell actually does

Start with temperature, because that is the variable with the longest research behind it. A British Journal of Dermatology paper on local temperature variation and the sebum excretion rate heated and cooled forehead skin and found the surface rate shifting by something like ten percent for every degree. The authors were careful to say that what a surface measurement captures may not match what the gland is doing several millimetres down, and that caution is worth carrying forward. The direction, though, has held up.

More recently, a randomised crossover trial published in Environmental Research in 2025 exposed volunteers to temperature swings and measured what happened to skin. At 32 degrees, sebum secretion was significantly higher at both sixty and a hundred and twenty minutes; cold exposure did something different and worse, reducing barrier integrity. Earlier work points the same way over longer spans: Piérard-Franchimont and colleagues, writing on seasonal modulation of sebum excretion in Dermatologica in 1990, treated the influence of ambient temperature on the excretion rate as generally accepted.

The helmet's own contribution is not heat production. It is occlusion. At 80 percent ambient humidity there is hardly an evaporation gradient to begin with (the wet-season piece makes this point about the blow-dry), and a polycarbonate shell with foam pads removes the little gradient that was left. Sweat cannot go anywhere. So it stays. Add the strap under the jaw and the pads at the temples, and a specific pattern of irritation shows up along exactly those lines.

The effect is strongest where the helmet covers most fully. A paper on the physiological characteristics of scalp skin with high sebum production measured Asian participants reporting an oily scalp with dandruff, and it found the scalp proper carrying more sebum, less hydration and weaker barrier function than either the frontal hairline or the nape. In practice that is the crown: the inside of the lid, in other words.

Then the limit, stated plainly, because this guide would rather name a gap than paper over one. Nobody has put a sebumeter on a head coming off a rented Vision on Nguyễn Văn Thoại. The protocols above involve forearms, foreheads and climate chambers. What transfers is the mechanism: warm skin under an occlusive cover produces more sebum and cannot shed the sweat that comes with it, and a helmet in this city is warm and occlusive for as long as you are moving.

The itch is grease plus a strap, and they want different answers

Two distinct irritations arrive together, which explains why people describe the feeling poorly and are then offered the wrong treatment. The first is biological. A comprehensive review of seborrhoeic dermatitis and dandruff estimates that the two conditions together affect roughly half of all adults, and it attributes the pathogenesis to sebaceous secretion, fungal colonisation of the skin surface, individual susceptibility and the interactions among them. Malassezia, the yeast concerned, lacks the machinery to build its own fatty acids, so it relies on the lipids it finds on your skin. More sebum, more substrate.

The second is mechanical and much simpler: friction. Foam pads at the temples, a nylon strap along the jaw, a liner that never fully dries between rides, and sweat salt drying in place over four hours of riding. That part has nothing to do with yeast and will not respond to anything in the pot.

There is one study that goes at the helmet question directly, and it deserves both a mention and a warning label. A conference paper on construction workers in Karangploso reports that wearing a helmet more than six hours a day significantly raises the risk of dandruff, with the authors proposing raised scalp temperature and humidity driving sebaceous activity and microbial proliferation. It is an abstract from an occupational sample on a building site, and I have not seen its sample size or design. Treat it as the only direct measurement anybody has published rather than as a number to build a rule on. The mechanism underneath it is far better evidenced than the paper itself.

Here is what the basin does. It strips the lipid film, the substrate, and that is why relief after a proper herbal wash is real and immediate, and also why it does not last. That relief holds as long as you stay off the bike, which on a riding holiday comes to about a day. The frequency piece makes the general case for a short interval, and a rider is the strongest form of that argument on the site.

The mechanical half leads to two requests. In a good gội đầu room, hands work with the pads of the fingers, which is the whole craft; in a rushed room at a busy hour, fingernails. Those are the wrong tool for a scalp already raw at the strap line, and đừng cào bằng móng tay nhé asks for no fingernails. Should a patch be genuinely sore, point at it before you recline and say chỗ này đang rát, nhẹ thôi nhé. With your head in the cradle and below the level of your body, you cannot see what is coming.

On the helmet itself, the one input you control, my view is simple: buy your own. A rental liner has been damp since the renter before you, and it does not dry in this climate; yours can sit in the sun on a balcony between rides. It is the cheapest line in a Da Nang trip budget, and the only one that changes what shows up at the basin.

The second wash is the one you came for, and the one that gets cut

A Vietnamese wash comes in two passes, and that structure matters more to a greasy scalp than to any other guest in the room. The first pass takes the surface off. In the second, the decoction actually sits, and most of the massage happens there as well. The longer menus write both in. On a busy evening at the cheap end, a tired technician saves four minutes by turning the second pass into a rinse, and that one economy wrecks the booking for you specifically. Saying Gội hai lần giúp mình nhé at the counter rather than mid-lather costs nothing and fixes it.

The second pass is not optional, and chemistry explains why. Bồ kết, the roasted locust pod that forms the base of most Da Nang blends, is a saponin cleanser. It foams weakly by design and lifts sebum perfectly well, but it does a poor job breaking down silicone and styling wax. The herb breakdown covers the rest of the roster. A weak lather is a feature here, since the last thing an irritated scalp needs is an aggressive surfactant, yet the feature comes with a condition: it needs the dwell and the second pass to do in two gentle rounds what one harsh shampoo does in one.

Some of what goes into the pot targets your problem directly. Green tea, trà xanh, turns up in blends intended for oily scalps. Pomelo peel and lemongrass, vỏ bưởi and sả, provide the astringency and most of the scent. A few houses add lime. Ask trong nồi có trà xanh không ạ? and you will hear about green tea and, more usefully, find out within about two seconds whether the house brews anything at all or pours from a bottle.

Next comes the request that sounds backwards on a menu advertising a nourishing mask. Keep it off your scalp. A hair mask is formulated for fibre and belongs from the mid-length down; put at the roots of a scalp already producing too much oil, it quietly undoes the fifteen minutes you just paid for. Đừng thoa dầu xả lên da đầu nhé is an ordinary salon request, and nobody will take it personally. If you also turned up with clay or pomade in your hair, the short-hair piece deals with that case, and it applies with more force when the wax sits on top of four hours of sweat.

Hold on to the blow-dry, for a reason that applies to riders in particular. Walk out with a damp scalp, put a shell over it an hour later, and you are back to warm occluded skin, exactly the state you just paid someone to interrupt. Dry hair under a helmet is not a luxury on this itinerary. Whether the wash lasts a day or an evening depends on it.

Buy the short tier. Buy it twice

These are the bands, taken from the houses in this city that publish a menu. Across four of them, a 25-to-30-minute wash costs 120K to 350K. Forty-five minutes is 250K to 550K. An hour runs 380K to 650K across all six. For seventy to ninety minutes, with steam, stones, facial work and a mask layered in, five houses charge 450K to 900K. The price page lists every house and source.

The case for the bottom of that table is simple, and it depends on where the cleansing happens: identically, within the first fifteen minutes of every tier. Pay for ninety minutes and the extra seventy-five buy a foot soak, a face, warm stones, herbal steam, a mask and tea. All good things. Not one of them removes sebum from your crown. You would be paying a premium for minutes directed at parts of your body other than the one bothering you.

This contradicts something already published on this site, and I would rather admit it than hope nobody notices. The session-length piece argues that the short wash is the expensive one, because the fixed overhead of greeting, reclining, towelling and drying takes a disproportionate share of a 25-minute slot. That holds when you are buying the massage, which is what most guests buy. When you are buying the wash it is wrong, since the wash is the single segment on the menu that scales worst with added time.

So run the arithmetic over a riding week rather than an evening. Two short washes land somewhere between 240K and 700K at band rates and give you two clean starts, placed on the two days you ride furthest. One long ritual at 450K to 900K gives you one clean start plus an hour of genuinely lovely things. If the hour is what you want, buy the hour with your eyes open; if what you want is to stop feeling like your own scalp belongs to somebody else, the money goes further split in two.

Drop one line item entirely, and it happens to be the one everybody wants: the herbal steam. These are the most pleasant ten minutes on any menu in Da Nang, and exactly the wrong ten minutes for a head that has spent the afternoon at 35 degrees inside foam. You are trying to get out of warm occluded air, not back into it. Put the money toward the shoulder and neck segment instead, because that is where four hours in a riding position has done you the other kind of damage.

Ride first, then wash, and never the reverse

A scheduling mistake comes up often enough to earn a section of its own. People book the head spa for the morning of the Hải Vân day, since that was the slot the house had free. You then spend ninety minutes and most of a good budget getting your scalp clean and dry, and after that four hours sealing it under a damp liner at altitude in the sun. The wash goes at the end of a riding day. Every time.

That order suits the trade here anyway: evening is when these rooms are busy and when walk-ins are easiest to place. The booking piece explains how far ahead you need to message and when you can simply turn up. Park on the pavement outside (every house on the street expects it, and nobody will comment), then leave the helmet with the shoes.

Geography has an easy answer on this itinerary. In the beach cluster of Mỹ An and An Thượng, the rental shops and the English-language menus share the same few streets, exactly what a rider wants at 19:00 with a sore neck. Hải Châu, around Hàn Market, is cheaper and serves a more local clientele; expect more pointing, and better prices for it. The neighbourhood guide weighs the two. This guide's pick, Reborn Nails & Retreat, is on Nguyễn Văn Thoại in An Thượng, lists a 25-minute wash at 120K and has English on the floor, which counts for more than usual when you have four requests to make.

A final branch, for anyone tempted by the Korean-style version with the scalp camera. The comparison page explains what each visit is for. Point a probe at a crown that has spent a week under a helmet and you get a genuinely vivid image: a shiny film, plugged follicles, the works, and the picture will be accurate. None of it comes off that way, though. Given one free afternoon and a greasy scalp, the photograph is the souvenir and the basin is the purchase.

Frequently asked

Does wearing a motorbike helmet cause dandruff?

Only one direct measurement has been published: a conference paper on construction workers in Karangploso, which found a significantly raised risk of dandruff above six hours a day in a helmet and proposed that raised scalp temperature and humidity drive sebaceous activity and microbial growth. Because it is an abstract from an occupational sample, read it as a signal rather than a settled finding. The mechanism behind it is better evidenced than the study: sebum output climbs with skin temperature, Malassezia relies on that sebum since it cannot make its own fatty acids, and a padded shell provides warmth and occlusion for as long as you wear it.

Why does my hair get greasy so fast in Vietnam?

Mostly heat, plus the fact that you are probably on a scooter. Locally heating skin has been measured to raise surface sebum by roughly ten percent per degree; a 2025 crossover trial in Environmental Research found significant rises after an hour at 32 degrees; and Da Nang delivers 30-degree afternoons at 75 to 85 percent humidity, so there is almost no evaporation to carry sweat off. That is why Vietnamese salons wash two and three times a week. Znews ran the same explanation, relayed from the travel firm Lanytrip, for why Chinese visitors were queuing for Vietnamese basins.

Will a head spa fix an oily, itchy scalp?

Yes for the grease, and fast: the double herbal wash strips the lipid film that feeds the yeast behind most flaking. The itch gets partly better, because some of it comes from friction with the strap and the pads rather than from sebum. As for the cause, no. Relief lasts while you stay off the bike, and on a riding holiday that is roughly a day. Ask them to keep conditioner and the hair mask off your scalp and to do the second wash in full rather than as a rinse; if anything is sore, request finger pads instead of fingernails.

Which head spa tier is best for an oily scalp?

The 25-to-30-minute wash, which costs 120K to 350K across the four Da Nang houses that publish a figure for it; the guide's pick lists 25 minutes at 120K, the floor of that band. All the cleansing in every tier happens in the first fifteen minutes, so the steam, stones, facial work and nourishing mask in the 450K-to-900K rituals add nothing for this particular problem. Over a week of riding, two short washes beat one long ritual. Above all, skip the herbal steam: more warm occluded air is exactly what you are trying to escape.
