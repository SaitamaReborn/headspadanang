The pot is the safe part: a sensitive scalp, a fragrance allergy, and a Da Nang basin

Whether to book a Vietnamese herbal head spa with a reactive scalp or a fragrance allergy · what the patch-test registries say about the citral in lemongrass, why the bottled mask is the least transparent thing in the hour, the one step worth declining, and the sentence that has to come from you.

In short
  • Nobody at the counter will ask. Nhà báo Công Luận, in a report on the trade's growth, quoted Nguyễn Thế Nam of the traditional medicine department at Hanoi's Rehabilitation Hospital, who tells customers to state their own health condition to the technician before the session begins and to watch how the body responds during and after. The instruction is right and it leaves the work to you, because a Vietnamese salon runs on conversation instead of an intake form.
  • Carry one word: sả. Lemongrass oil is mostly citral, measured at 61 to 76 percent of the oil in a study of Cymbopogon citratus culm and leaf. Dermatology-clinic denominators, not holiday ones.
  • Our position goes against instinct: the simmered pot is the least risky thing in the room, and the bottled mask the most. A review of contact allergy to essential oils found that most reactions came from pure oils and high-concentration products, which a decoction is not. The question nobody at the counter is going to ask

The booking usually stalls at the same point. Somebody whose scalp reacts to things (eczema along the hairline, a fragrance allergy written on a dermatologist's letter, one bad fortnight after a salon treatment years ago) opens a Da Nang menu promising roasted locust pod, pomelo peel and lemongrass, and cannot decide if ninety minutes of that is a treat or a mistake. The word herbal sounds gentle. It also describes a list of plant oils that go onto the one piece of skin you cannot look at afterwards.

Vietnamese coverage of the trade is more useful on this than anything written for visitors. Nhà báo Công Luận, the paper of the Vietnam Journalists' Association, published a cautionary piece on gội đầu dưỡng sinh as the service spread through Hanoi spas and salons. It quotes Nguyễn Thế Nam, of the traditional medicine department at Hanoi's Rehabilitation Hospital, who starts where the trade's own marketing starts, with the head as the place the yang meridians converge, and then says what the marketing leaves out: acupressure from untrained hands in the wrong place can hurt, and essential oils and herbs of unclear origin, along with tools that have not been properly cleaned, can cause scalp irritation, dermatitis, hair loss or bacterial infection. He advises customers to check that a house is licensed and its technicians trained, to declare their health condition before the session begins, and to monitor how they feel during it and afterwards.

That middle instruction is the one to act on, and it is the only part of the visit nobody will prompt you for. A Vietnamese salon runs on talk, not paperwork. The prices are on a card, the tiers get pointed at, and the next thing that happens is a bed with a basin cut into the end of it. Translation effort in this city has gone into the menu rather than the medical history: the iPad at the guide's pick in An Thượng carries twenty languages, which is a serious investment in being understood, and none of those twenty will ask what you cannot have on your head.

So say it standing up, at the counter, while the technician is still somebody you are facing rather than a pair of hands above your forehead. Mình bị da đầu nhạy cảm is my scalp is sensitive. Mình bị dị ứng tinh dầu is I am allergic to essential oils, and if you know which one, name it: sả is lemongrass, vỏ bưởi is pomelo peel, gừng is ginger. Then the request that follows, cho mình xem thành phần nhé, let me see the ingredients. None of it reads as difficult in a Vietnamese salon; the etiquette piece covers the rest of the counter vocabulary, and the walkthrough sets out what happens once you are horizontal.

What is in the water, and why it is the safe part

Start with the pot, since it is the thing people are frightened of and it deserves the least of the fear. A Da Nang decoction is dried bồ kết pods, roasted and then simmered for fifteen to thirty minutes until the water turns amber and raises a low foam of its own, usually with pomelo peel, lemongrass and sometimes green tea alongside them. The herb breakdown gives the full roster. What reaches your head is water that spent half an hour over a flame with plant matter in it, poured out of a thermos. It is dilute, in other words, and nobody's formulation.

The reference work on this risk is de Groot and Schmidt's 2016 review of essential oils and contact allergy in the journal Dermatitis, and its headline count is higher than most people expect: close to eighty essential oils have caused contact allergy. Fifty-five of them have been tested in consecutive patients suspected of contact dermatitis, and nine gave more than 2 percent positive patch tests: laurel, turpentine, orange, tea tree, citronella, ylang-ylang, sandalwood, clove and costus root. Citronella deserves a pause. It is a Cymbopogon, the same genus as the lemongrass that simmers in the pot.

The sentence in that review that should govern your booking, however, is the one on dose. Most reactions, the authors write, are caused by the application of pure oils or high-concentration products. A herbal rinse is at the opposite end of the scale. The neat end does exist on a Da Nang menu: it is the small bottle of tinh dầu warmed between somebody's palms and worked into a scalp during the longer tiers, and that is a different proposition from the jug.

One more line from the same review reframes the room usefully: occupational contact dermatitis may occur in professionals performing massages. The hands in the water meet these oils thirty times a week, and you meet them once. A house that has been brewing the same decoction for years with the same staff is running an informal exposure test every working day, and that is a weak signal rather than a guarantee, but it is more information than the laminated card on the counter gives you.

Then there is the gap, which this guide prefers to name than skate past. Nobody has patch tested a gội đầu dưỡng sinh decoction. The same authors note that relevance data for essential-oil patch testing are generally missing or inadequate, and that patients are best tested with their own products: a polite way of saying the literature cannot tell you what one particular Vietnamese family blend will do to one particular scalp.

Citral, the one name worth carrying into the room

If you take one molecule away from this piece, take citral. Lemongrass oil is mostly made of it: a study that extracted the oil from the culm and the leaf of Cymbopogon citratus found citral to be the major component, at 61.20 to 76.46 percent. Citral itself is two aldehydes, geranial and neral, and is one of the components of Fragrance Mix II, the screening marker that patch-test clinics use for the less classic fragrance allergens.

The best numbers are in a 2020 paper in Contact Dermatitis by Hagvall and colleagues, who patch tested 1476 dermatitis patients with geranial, neral and citral alongside the Swedish baseline series. Positive reactions were 2.9 percent to citral, 3.4 percent to geranial and 1.9 percent to neral; together, citral and geranial gave 4.2 percent positives in consecutive dermatitis patients. Of those who reacted to citral or its components, between a quarter and a third also reacted to Fragrance Mix II and 61 percent to oxidised geraniol, and the authors concluded that testing with citral itself catches allergy the baseline series misses.

Two other series show the same molecule in a harsher light. A five-year study across 23 Spanish centres, published in Contact Dermatitis in 2021, gave a specific fragrance series to 1013 patients who had already reacted to a baseline fragrance marker, and citral was the most frequent allergen in occupational eczema at 20.5 percent, with a large share of hand and facial dermatitis attached to it. In Melbourne, a review of 964 patients diagnosed with allergic contact dermatitis found that citral was the single most frequently occurring allergen the Australian baseline series does not include. An older Lund study of 586 consecutive hand eczema patients tested with citral at 2 percent found 28 positive reactions, plus 82 patients who had an irritant reaction and no allergy at all, and this is the finding that matters to anyone whose scalp objects to everything with no diagnosis behind it. Citral can irritate skin that is not sensitised to it.

Read the denominators honestly. Every figure above is from a dermatology clinic, meaning from people who turned up with a rash and were referred for testing. The rate among holidaymakers who have never had a skin problem is lower than any of those percentages, probably by a lot, and nobody has measured it. Ranking is what the numbers are good for: of the plants that end up in a Vietnamese pot, lemongrass is the one with a documented sensitiser in it at high concentration, and that is worth one sentence at the counter.

Here is the part that reverses the intuition. Under the European cosmetics rules, 26 fragrance substances must be named on a product label once they pass 0.001 percent in a leave-on product or 0.01 percent in a rinse-off one, and citral is on the list. So the bottled shampoo is legally obliged to tell you about the molecule, while the family decoction simmering in the back cannot tell you anything, because it has no label and no two batches are identical. The herbal option is both the transparent-sounding one and the opaque one. A citral-sensitive guest is left with a question and a refusal: trong nồi có sả không ạ?, is there lemongrass in the pot, and a no to the herbal head steam, where lemongrass does most of its work and arrives hot.

The bottle beside the pot

Count what actually touches your scalp in an hour. There is the decoction, yes, and in most houses a commercial shampoo for the first pass, a conditioner, a hair mask in the signature tiers, sometimes a scalp serum or a tonic sprayed on before the dry. Those are formulated products, and the allergens that appear in registries live in them. The pot gets the suspicion while the bottles get waved through.

The preservative to know is the isothiazolinone family. Reeder and colleagues, writing in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2024, analysed 9028 patients patch tested in North America between 2017 and 2020 and found 21.9 percent positive to at least one isothiazolinone, with methylisothiazolinone alone at 14.4 percent. European numbers from general dermatitis populations are lower and move with regulation: around 1.5 percent in a Danish hospital cohort of 2536 patients tested between 2006 and 2010, and roughly 2.1 percent across the German IVDK network over a decade. All of those are referral populations again, and the North American figure reflects an expanded panel rather than a worse continent. The ranking is what travels: this is a common contact allergen in rinse-off cosmetics, and a head spa is an hour of rinse-off cosmetics.

The surfactant worth knowing is cocamidopropyl betaine, the foaming agent in a great many gentle shampoos, which a North American study of 975 patch-tested patients in 2001 found positive in 15 of them. Vietnamese hospital patient material names the same two culprits: the contact dermatitis page published by Nguyễn Tri Phương Hospital lists isothiazolinone preservatives and cocamidopropyl betaine among the shampoo ingredients that cause trouble. European registries and a Ho Chi Minh City hospital handout agreeing on the same two molecules is about as much consensus as this subject offers.

Which makes the useful request a physical one rather than a verbal one. Cho mình xem chai dầu gội nhé asks to see the shampoo bottle, and the ingredient lists on cosmetics sold in Vietnam are printed in INCI, the same Latin and English nomenclature used on the bottle in your bathroom at home, so you can read it even though you cannot read the label around it. Two minutes at the counter beats a conversation about percentages.

Now the blunt position, since this guide likes to have one. At the top of the price table, the nourishing hair mask is the least transparent thing in the hour and, for a scalp that reacts, the least necessary thing in it. It is also the step most likely to go on the roots instead of the mid-lengths, which is wrong for everyone and worse for you: đừng thoa dầu xả lên da đầu nhé keeps conditioner and mask off the scalp, and the helmet-hair piece makes the same request for another reason.

The steam pot is the only thing in the room that can burn you

One hazard on a head spa menu has nothing to do with chemistry, and the Vietnamese press has covered it better than the English-language web. The same Công Luận piece relays an account that had circulated on Facebook: a woman was having a herbal head steam when the steam pot gave way, leaving her burned behind the left ear, on the neck and across the upper torso by high-pressure hot steam, and admitted to hospital with a swollen ear canal and eardrum, blistering, and acute inflammation of both the outer and middle ear. Nguyễn Thế Nam's comment in that article covers the mechanism in general terms: machines that do not meet standards, or that are operated outside the correct procedure, lead to fire, explosion and thermal burns.

Hold that at its real weight. It is one reported incident with a social-media origin, relayed by a newspaper, and no rate attaches to it. Da Nang houses steam heads all day without anybody being hurt. What it does establish is where the risk sits in the room, which is not in the herbs and not in the hands but in the one piece of pressurised equipment on the menu, parked behind your head while you lie in a position that makes it impossible to see. If the ear detail troubles you, the ear-cleaning piece is the other half of that argument.

A scalp that is already irritated has a second reason to skip the step, and it is unrelated to equipment. Steam is heat plus occlusion, the direction you move in when you want more sebum and more inflammation and not less, and in a city that sits at 75 to 85 percent humidity for most of the year your scalp already has quite enough of both. The wet-season piece makes this argument about drying; here the same physics is pointed at a flare. The refusal is four words: Mình không xông hơi đầu nhé.

That refusal changes which tier you should buy, and the arithmetic is worth doing before you book rather than at the counter. The herbal steam is one of the things the 70-to-90-minute band exists to deliver, priced at 450K to 900K across the five houses that publish those tiers. Take the steam out and a good part of the premium goes with it. The hour, at 380K to 650K across all six houses, carries the double wash, the decoction dwell and the full neck and shoulder sequence, which is the part of a head spa a sensitive scalp can enjoy without negotiating. The price page lists the bands and the houses behind each one.

Buy the cheap wash as a test dose

The usual advice to a visitor with one head spa in the budget is to spend it on the long ritual, and for a reactive scalp it is backwards. Book the short wash first. Four houses in this city publish a 25-to-30-minute wash at 120K to 350K, and the guide's pick lists 25 minutes at 120K, which gets you the two shampoos, the hands and the full ingredient exposure at the cheapest price in Da Nang. Put it on day two. If nothing happens, the 80-minute Signature at 500K is still there on day six, and you will book it with something you did not know on arrival.

Calling that a test dose is a convenience rather than a claim, and the difference matters. A real patch test is a measured concentration held against skin under an occlusive chamber and read by a dermatologist, conventionally at day three or four, because allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed reaction that takes a day or more to declare itself. Which is the actual argument for the sequence: the value is not in the short wash, it is in the 48 hours you leave after it. Two head spas on consecutive afternoons tell you nothing, since the second one lands before the first one has finished speaking.

Some scalps should stay out of the basin altogether this week, and Vietnamese houses say so in the guidance they publish: an open wound, an active scalp infection or fungus, a fever. Add to those the 48 hours after colour or a perm, which the colour piece covers at length. Nguyễn Thế Nam's list of people who should ask a doctor first runs to low blood pressure, cervical spine degeneration, pregnancy and chronic illness, and the first and second of those concern the massage and not the herbs, as the neck piece and the pregnancy piece explain.

Where to go depends on how much you need to say. Four requests in a row is a lot to mime, so here the beach cluster of Mỹ An and An Thượng earns its premium, with English on more floors than anywhere else in the city; Hải Châu around Hàn Market is cheaper and will cost you in pointing. The neighbourhood guide weighs the two. There is one branch to consider before booking: the Korean-style studios apply more products and run more equipment than a Vietnamese room, scalp cameras and ampoules and machine massage, and the comparison page says what each visit is for. A reactive scalp wants the shorter ingredient list, and in Da Nang that is the jug of brown water.

If one word goes into the room with you, make it sả.

Frequently asked

Can I get a head spa if I have a sensitive scalp?

Usually yes, with three changes to the booking. Buy the 25-to-30-minute wash first rather than the long ritual, because it costs 120K to 350K across the four Da Nang houses that publish it and carries the same ingredient exposure as the expensive tiers. Leave 48 hours before anything longer, since allergic contact dermatitis is delayed and patch tests are conventionally read at day three or four. And decline the herbal head steam, which is heat plus occlusion on skin that is already inflamed. Say what you react to at the counter while you are still standing up, because nobody will ask: a Vietnamese salon runs on conversation rather than an intake form.

Can you be allergic to lemongrass oil?

Yes, and the reason is citral, which is most of lemongrass oil. One study of Cymbopogon citratus found citral at 61 to 76 percent of the oil from the culm and leaf. In a Swedish study of 1476 dermatitis patients, citral and geranial together produced 4.2 percent positive patch tests, while a Spanish series of 1013 fragrance-tested patients had citral as the most frequent allergen in occupational eczema at 20.5 percent. These are dermatology-clinic figures, and the rate among ordinary travellers is much lower. Citral can irritate skin that is not allergic to it, too. Ask trong noi co sa khong a, is there lemongrass in the pot, and skip the herbal steam, where lemongrass concentrates.

Can you get a head spa with eczema or psoriasis?

Not on broken or weeping skin, and not in an active flare. Vietnamese houses themselves publish the same contraindications: open wounds, active scalp infection or fungus, a fever. Between flares it is a judgement call that your dermatologist is better placed to make than a guide, and the version to request is the plain wash with no essential oil, no steam and no mask, and conditioner kept off the scalp. The traditional-medicine doctor whom Nha bao Cong Luan quoted on this trade advises declaring your condition before the session and watching how your skin responds afterwards, which is the right order.

Is the herbal steam at a Vietnamese head spa safe?

It is the only part of the menu that puts pressurised equipment behind your head, and Nha bao Cong Luan relayed one circulated account of a steam pot that failed during a head steam, leaving the guest with hot-steam burns behind the ear, on the neck and the upper torso as well as acute ear inflammation. One reported incident is not a rate, and houses here steam heads all day without harm. Our position is to decline it anyway if you have a reactive scalp, for the duller reason: heat and occlusion push a flare the wrong way. Removing it also takes a good part of the premium out of the 70-to-90-minute tiers, priced at 450K to 900K across five houses.
