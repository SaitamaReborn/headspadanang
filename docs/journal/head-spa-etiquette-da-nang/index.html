Nobody will correct you: head spa etiquette in Da Nang, from the shoe rack to the tip

How to behave inside a Da Nang head spa · the two words of Vietnamese that do the work of a phrasebook, why a quiet guest gets the worse hour, what the tip should be in đồng rather than percent, and how you pay at a counter with a QR code and no card reader.

In short
  • Silence costs you more than any wrong move with your shoes. A Da Nang floor reads a quiet guest as a satisfied one, and you are lying with your eyes shut while the technician stands somewhere past the top of your head, so pressure that was wrong at minute six is still wrong at minute fifty. Nhẹ hơn ạ for lighter, mạnh hơn ạ for firmer, and ạ on the end of anything turns it polite.
  • Tip in đồng, never in percent. Vietnamese sources call tipping tùy tâm, according to your heart, and the retail software firm Sapo puts the share of Vietnamese people with no tipping habit at around 70 percent in its own guide. Spa recruitment ads advertise a base of 6 to 10 million a month with tip 100% thuộc nhân viên printed next to it. 50K after a short wash, 100K after an hour, into the hand rather than onto the till.
  • Carry small notes. Vietnam settles its bills by QR code: NAPAS cleared 9.56 billion transactions in 2024 with VietQR transfers up 118 percent, while ATM withdrawals fell 19.5 percent in volume. The code by the till wants a Vietnamese bank app you do not have, and a neighbourhood house is not where foreign cards work.
  • Sort all of it out while standing at the counter: pressure, steam, how much time you really have, whether you intend to film. For the houses on the price table, a 25 to 30 minute wash runs 120K to 350K and the hour 380K to 650K. Those sentences change the hour. They do not change the price.

The ritual reaches you by video now

A decade ago, gội đầu dưỡng sinh reached you through a Vietnamese friend, or through whoever cut your hair. These days you meet it on a phone. Search the platforms for Da Nang and the clips climb to hundreds of thousands of likes, and the captions sell the thing almost purely on arithmetic: the viral fifteen-pound Vietnam head spa, a ninety-minute twenty-one-step ritual, a fifteen-dollar afternoon that one creator reckons would cost two hundred at home. The trouble is that those numbers are broadly right. So people turn up at a counter in An Thượng with the sequence memorised shot by shot and no idea how to behave once inside it.

A video cannot show you that this is not spa etiquette. It is household etiquette. A neighbourhood house in Mỹ An is a small family business, run more or less the way a Vietnamese home is: the pot of bồ kết and pomelo peel simmering in plain view, somebody's mother at the till, three technicians who have worked side by side for years. The rules worth knowing are the ones you would want as a guest in the flat upstairs. Nobody will say any of them aloud, since explaining them to an adult would be the rude part.

Read the doorway, then, instead of asking. A rack by the step, or sandals in a loose pile next to a tray of plastic slides, means shoes off and slides on. If the floor is tiled and staff cross it in trainers, keep yours. Get this wrong and it is a trivial offence that nobody will correct; still, those five seconds of looking separate arriving as a guest from arriving as a customer. Keep your bag beside the bed, within arm's reach: houses this size have no lockers, and nobody expects a stranger to watch over your passport. The neighbourhood guide covers which streets this describes.

The clock belongs in the same paragraph. These houses sell slots and book them back to back, so a guest who turns up fifteen minutes late does not get an hour finishing fifteen minutes later. They get forty-five minutes at the hour's price, and often nobody mentions that is what happened. If the traffic on Võ Nguyên Giáp has eaten your margin, send a message before you are late rather than an apology after. How booking actually works here has the rest of it.

The expensive mistake is silence

This is the one worth caring about, yet no list of travel-blog do's and don'ts mentions it. You will lie on a giường gội đầu, a padded bed around 190 cm long, with your neck in a basin cradle, your eyes shut and a technician standing beyond the crown of your head. She cannot see your face from there. Pressure, for her, comes from the feel of your scalp and from whatever you tell her, and on most foreign visits that is nothing at all.

On Vietnamese service floors a quiet guest counts as a contented one. With domestic customers that is correct, since domestic customers speak up. Visitors conclude instead that speaking mid-ritual would be impolite, so an hour bought for 380K to 650K is spent courteously waiting out a pressure they dislike. At minute six, forty-five seconds of clumsy Vietnamese buys the other fifty-four. Nothing else on offer comes at that price.

Two things sit next to that, and where the line falls between them matters. Small corrections go on the bed: pressure, water temperature, a hand that keeps snagging an earring. She treats those as adjustments, made inside a second with no loss of rhythm. Structural changes do not go on the bed: dropping the herbal steam, skipping the tẩy da đầu scalp scrub, switching tier, an hour you now have to leave after forty minutes. Every one of those re-plans the session. A technician who re-plans halfway takes the minutes for it out of your scalp time, so say all of it at the counter, upright, before anyone turns on a tap.

The reverse also holds, and almost nobody believes it: you are allowed an hour of saying nothing at all. Vietnamese salon culture is sociable and the counter will gladly chat, but the dưỡng sinh frame is a quiet one. If a technician stops talking two minutes in, she has not taken offence. She has read you right and gone back to work.

Two words of Vietnamese, and the particle that does the rest

There is no neutral word for you in Vietnamese. People address each other by relative age, so the woman washing your hair calls you chị or anh, older sister or older brother, and calls herself em, the younger one. Your vocative for the hour comes from that. If she is younger than you, Em ơi gets her attention; if older, chị ơi. In a trade staffed mostly by women in their twenties, the first is rarely wrong. Then add ạ. Put that single syllable on the end of any sentence and you have most of Vietnamese politeness, and a foreigner who uses it is treated measurably better for the rest of the visit.

The working vocabulary is six phrases long. Nhẹ hơn ạ, lighter. Mạnh hơn ạ, firmer. Đau quá, that hurts, which needs no softening and should be said the instant it is true, especially across the neck where the bấm huyệt pressure points sit. Vậy là được rồi ạ means that is right where I want it, and it is the most useful of the lot because it tells her to stay there instead of carrying on hunting. Nóng quá for water too hot. Cảm ơn em nhé at the end. All six are learnable on the Grab ride over.

For anything beyond a phrase, use the translation app and hold the screen out. Reviewers of Da Nang houses describe exactly that going fine, and the trade is entirely used to it. What it is not used to is a paragraph of spoken English at speed. A few counters do run in English. At the house this guide picks, Reborn Nails & Retreat in An Thượng, the owner Fiona works the floor in it and the menu is on an iPad in twenty languages; that takes the problem out of the one conversation where a misunderstanding costs real money, the one about which tier you have just bought. The tip, settled

In English, this question gets you a shrug pointing four ways. Travel guides set a spa tip at 50,000 to 100,000 đồng, or at 10 percent, or at 20, or say none is expected; one Ho Chi Minh City listing cites a mandatory minimum of 150,000. The Vietnamese-language answer is steadier, and more useful too. Tipping here is tùy tâm, which translates as according to your heart and means genuinely optional. Sapo, the retail software company whose terminals sit on plenty of these counters, puts it bluntly in its own guide: not obligatory, no habit of it among roughly 70 percent of Vietnamese people, and the generous ones are foreign visitors.

Your number should be set by where the money lands. Recruitment ads for gội đầu dưỡng sinh technicians (the ones running in Ho Chi Minh City) offer a base of 6 to 10 million đồng a month plus commission, and they print tip 100% thuộc nhân viên, the whole tip to the technician, as a perk worth advertising. In Da Nang the ads more often say làm ăn chia, revenue share. Divide 6 to 10 million over a 26-day month and a day's base pay comes to somewhere near 230K to 385K: roughly what one 60-minute ritual costs the person on the bed.

Hence the number, and the unit. 50K after a 25-minute wash, 100K after an hour or more, 200K if somebody gave you ninety minutes and you intend to come back. Percentages are an import and they warp badly down here: 20 percent of a 900K signature is 180K, most of a day's advertised base, handed over for one hour of work the house has already charged 900K for. No Vietnamese guest does that, and the habit, repeated along a street, changes which customers technicians want. Give it to the technician instead of leaving it at the till, which takes one gesture and the words cái này cho em nhé, this is for you, and do it after the blow-dry rather than on arrival, since a tip offered up front reads as an instruction.

A mandatory minimum tip is a price dressed in somebody else's word. Pay what the menu says, decline the surcharge, and tip what you would have tipped anyway. The one bill really worth questioning sits at the resort end of the city, where a service charge of 10 to 15 percent is the norm. A house in An Thượng or Hải Châu has no such line, and có tính thêm phí gì không ạ, is anything added, settles it at the counter in a single breath.

How the money actually moves

Vietnam has moved further from cash than most of the countries its visitors fly in from, and what replaced it is not a card. NAPAS, which clears the country's interbank payments, reported 9.56 billion transactions in 2024, a 29.7 percent rise in volume, and its instant transfer service made up more than 8.9 billion of them. The VietQR codes behind that service grew 118 percent year on year, to a third of those transfers; over the year-end peak the system was clearing more than 10 million QR transactions a day. ATM withdrawals went the opposite way. They fell 19.5 percent in volume and now make up 2.63 percent of everything NAPAS handles. In August 2026 NAPAS signed its QR product into KiotViet, the point-of-sale software that a great many small Vietnamese shops run their till on, and that is the screen most likely to be glowing at you when you pay.

None of it helps you. A VietQR code is scanned from inside a Vietnamese bank's app, and a visitor on a fortnight's trip has no such app. The laminated square by the till is for the local guests, a card machine is a maybe in An Thượng and a no across most of the city, and you will be paying cash. Bring it in small notes. A 500K note against a 380K ritual is fine and change will be found, but a tip wants a 50K or a 100K, and the technician who has just dried your hair has nothing to break it with. Draw the money before you walk over, in one go: the fee on a foreign card is charged per withdrawal rather than per đồng, so one larger draw beats four small ones.

One more thing about money, aimed at the end of the hour. The tea is free and so is the little plate that comes with it. The suggestion that arrives around minute fifty is not. If a technician proposes adding the scalp scrub or stretching the hour to eighty minutes, the answer is allowed to be thôi, lần sau nhé, not this time. The fair-rate table exists so you can tell whether what has just been offered is priced like the rest of the city or like a holiday.

Your clothes, your jewellery, and the camera

You keep your clothes on. There is no robe and no changing room, nothing to bring either, and the one clothing decision that matters is the collar. In the hour-long rituals the neck and shoulder work extends to the edge of the shoulder, so with a stiff collar or a high neckline the technician either works through fabric or asks you to pull it down. A soft wide neckline settles that before the question comes up. Some houses drape a wrap or a towel over your shoulders. Accept it.

Earrings come out, and long necklaces with them, because the basin cradle is precisely where a hoop snags and the rim is precisely where a pendant ends up in two inches of herbal water. Hair ties go on the shelf rather than round your wrist. Glasses have nowhere to live for an hour, so a case in your bag is worth more than it sounds. And if you are wearing makeup and have booked a ritual long enough to include a face cleanse or a warm towel, it is coming off. The people who did not know that are the ones who look startled at the mirror afterwards.

The real decision of the hour is your phone. Your hands are free throughout, and that is the trap: a scalp massage can be scrolled through from start to finish, plenty of people do exactly that, and the hour they paid for becomes an hour of what they do anyway, only with somebody's fingers in their hair. Airplane mode, face down, in the bag. Among the free things in this piece, nothing does more for what your 380K actually buys.

That leaves the camera, since most people reading this found the ritual through somebody else's. Filming a Da Nang head spa is ordinary these days, and the houses know it. Ask a counter politely and it will usually say yes, and sometimes hold the phone for you. Use those words when you ask, though: mình quay video được không ạ. That room is a workplace; the person in your frame is at work there, often on a revenue share, and by standing behind your head she did not agree to anybody's feed. My own view goes further, so take it or leave it: film thirty seconds at the start, then put the thing away. The hour is better unfilmed, and your clip of somebody's hands in your hair cannot be told apart from the four hundred already up.

Frequently asked

Do you tip at a head spa in Vietnam?

It is optional, and in đồng rather than as a percentage. Vietnamese sources describe tipping as tùy tâm, according to your heart; the retail software firm Sapo's own guide says it is not obligatory and that around 70 percent of Vietnamese people have no habit of it. What makes a tip worth giving is where it goes: spa recruitment ads list tip 100% thuộc nhân viên, the whole tip to the technician, next to a base of 6 to 10 million đồng a month. A reasonable scale is 50K after a 25-minute wash, 100K after an hour, 200K after ninety minutes. Hand it to the technician after the blow-dry, not to the till. Ignore the 20 percent advice on travel sites, and treat a mandatory minimum tip as a price rather than a tip.

What should I wear to a head spa in Da Nang?

Whatever you walked in wearing. You remain fully clothed, with no robe or changing room, and the neckline is the only choice worth making: in an hour-long ritual the neck and shoulder work reaches the edge of the shoulder, so with a soft wide neckline nobody has to ask you to pull a collar down. Remove earrings and any long necklace before lying back, since the basin cradle is where a hoop catches and the rim is where a pendant lands in the water. Bring a case for your glasses. Makeup will come off in any ritual that includes a face cleanse or a warm towel.

How do you pay at a head spa in Da Nang, and is it rude to ask the price first?

Cash in small notes, and asking is normal. These days Vietnam mostly pays by VietQR bank transfer, scanned from inside a Vietnamese banking app a visitor will not have, and neighbourhood houses rarely have card machines. Take money out before you go, because the foreign-card fee is charged per withdrawal, and set a 50K or 100K note aside for the tip, since at the end nobody will have change. At a Vietnamese counter it is ordinary to ask what a tier costs, and to ask có tính thêm phí gì không ạ, is anything added; the right moment is before you lie down.

Is it rude to ask for lighter or firmer pressure during a Vietnamese head spa?

No. The real mistake is keeping quiet. Standing beyond the top of your head, the technician cannot see your face, so she calibrates from your scalp and from whatever you say. Nhẹ hơn ạ means lighter, mạnh hơn ạ firmer, đau quá that hurts, and vậy là được rồi ạ means stay exactly there. Putting ạ at the end of anything makes it polite. Small corrections such as pressure and water temperature belong on the bed; bigger changes like dropping the steam, skipping the tẩy da đầu scalp scrub or shortening the session belong at the counter before the session starts, because re-planning it halfway comes out of your scalp time.
