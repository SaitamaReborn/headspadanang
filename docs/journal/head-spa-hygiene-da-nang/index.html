The brush, not the towel: head spa hygiene in a Da Nang basin

What is actually reused in that room

The thought usually comes late, somewhere between booking and the doorway. A stranger's hands, a basin that has held a hundred necks this month, a towel off a stack, and no idea what happened to any of it between the previous guest and you. Nobody asks the counter, partly because the question sounds rude in any language and mostly because no counter in any country gives an honest answer.

Make an inventory instead, since the list is short and it is not what people imagine. Water runs once and leaves. The herbal decoction is poured from a thermos and goes down the same drain. What stays in the room and meets the next head is the rim of the basin where your neck rests, the headrest, a towel, a wide-tooth comb for the wet comb-out, a round brush for the dry one, a sectioning clip, and, in houses that offer it, a metal ear pick. That makes six or seven items, of which three touch skin.

That inventory matters because Da Nang houses run the day as a line of back-to-back slots, which the etiquette piece covers from the other side, where you arrive late and lose fifteen minutes. A room turning over every hour from nine in the morning until eight at night puts thirty or so heads across one trolley, and whatever routine there is for wiping that trolley happens in the ninety seconds between a blow-dry and the next guest lying down. The walkthrough lays out the sequence those minutes have to fit into.

There is a body of dermatology research on infection caught in a salon, and it is more specific than the worry is. It has almost nothing to do with basins.

Every published outbreak starts at a barber's chair

The clearest recent account comes from Sardinia. Addari and colleagues, at the dermatology unit of the university hospital in Cagliari, published ten cases of tinea capitis in the Journal of Clinical Medicine in January 2025, all of them in male patients, median age 23.5, the youngest 17 and the oldest 68. To be included, a patient needed a clear history of a recent barber shave before the lesions appeared. Culture was run on three of the ten, and all three grew Trichophyton tonsurans. Two of the ten went on to scarring alopecia, which is permanent. One detail is worth keeping: the Wood's lamp, the ultraviolet light people picture a doctor waving over a scalp to settle this, was negative in every case.

The authors did not sample a single shop. Instead of demonstrating the routes they propose them, and the list in their paper names contaminated shaving equipment, the traumatic nature of haircut procedures, shared barber chairs and a lack of adherence to proper hygiene. Their recommendation is single-use equipment where possible and sterilised shaving tools between clients. They also write that their cases likely represent a fraction of the real total, and that sentence is what makes the series worth quoting rather than filing.

Look at what their comparison table contains and the pattern gets harder to miss. A German cohort of 18 patients, all male, median age 11.5, with a barbershop history in 18 of 18, infections sitting at the occiput, the neck and the beard. A Spanish outbreak of 107 patients, 99.1 percent male, median age 19.7, every one of them traced to a barbershop. A further series of 29, mostly boys. Same organism each time. The common exposure is not a wash. It is a blade or a clipper passed over skin that has just been abraded at the nape, where a short back and sides is finished.

That is why this piece exists in a head spa guide rather than a barbershop one. The Vietnamese men's shop around the corner from your hotel gives a razor finish on the neck, and often on the face, as part of the cut, which is the exposure the literature describes. A head spa holds no blade at all, with one exception: the metal pick used for lấy ráy tai. The ear-cleaning piece argues that step down on its own merits. Add this to its column: it is the only item on the menu that enters a canal and can graze the skin lining it. Mình không lấy ráy tai nhé removes it.

The one number for the thing that touches you

For the objects a head spa does use, the latest assessment is a short commentary by R. J. Hay of King's College London, published online on 27 January 2026 in the Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology. It accompanies a Copenhagen study by Shen and colleagues in the same journal, and the figures Hay takes from it are the only hard numbers in this whole subject: 27 percent of brushes and 4 percent of hair clippers were positive for pathogenic dermatophyte fungi. He notes that tools are often sterilised or cleaned intermittently and not after every customer, and that, given the difficulty of harmless sterilising processes, transmission remains a potential risk. On survival he is brief and useful. Arthrospores, the infecting particles, last long periods off a human or animal host, and heat above 60 degrees destroys them.

A second study he cites, from Port Harcourt in Nigeria, found fungi, dermatophytes among them, as well as bacteria on hairdressing equipment: combs, shavers, scissors. No percentages come with that one. Hay also declines to say how large the risk is in absolute terms, which is the right call and leaves the honest version in place: a measurable share of salon brushes carry something, and nobody has measured how often that share becomes an infected customer.

Vietnamese patient material reaches the same place from another direction. Hospital and pharmacy pages on nấm da đầu, scalp fungus, list shared combs, hats, towels and pillows as the way spores move between people, note that the fungus persists on dead skin and shed hair in damp surroundings, and say it does not travel through the air. Two literatures that never cite each other both name the comb first, which is about as much agreement as this subject produces.

So look at the trolley, not the laundry. Of everything on it, the brush is the item that drags across skin, keeps other people's hair between its bristles and gets rinsed least. In a Da Nang room it comes out twice, once wet and once under the dryer. Cho mình xin lược mới nhé asks for a clean comb and is a request rather than a question, which is the whole point: a request is settled in one second and in front of you. Better still, do what no guide tells you to do and put a brush in your bag. The curly-hair piece already argues for bringing your own on grounds of breakage, so the second reason costs nothing.

The towel is the wrong thing to stare at

Every hygiene checklist written for visitors to Vietnamese wash houses says the same, and most of them were written by wash houses: check that the towels look fresh and folded, check that the linen is changed for each guest. As advice it is not bad, but it concerns the one object in the room that carries the least information.

Here is why. Akhoundi and colleagues, in the Journal of Fungi in 2022, contaminated linen with the spores of three dermatophytes, including the T. tonsurans from those barbershop series, and ran it through domestic laundry. Their finding was that washing at 60 degrees removed the conidia. Heat drying in a household or laundromat machine did not, and neither did freezing. The variable that decides whether a towel is safe is therefore a dial setting in a back room, invisible from the bed, and no amount of folding reports on it.

Da Nang adds its own wrinkle, which belongs to the season this is published in. From September to December the city sits under long stretches of rain at 75 to 85 percent humidity, which is the argument for booking in the wet season in the first place, and also the reason laundry in this city dries indoors under a fan for most of a day. A towel can be properly washed and still arrive faintly damp, or be bone dry and have been washed cold. Smell tells you about the drying. Nothing in the room tells you about the wash.

The liquid half of the hour is the reassuring half, for a reason that has nothing to do with herbs. A Vietnamese decoction is dried bồ kết pods, pomelo peel and lemongrass simmered fifteen to thirty minutes, so the pot clears Hay's 60-degree line by a distance while it is hot, and the herb breakdown explains what else that time is doing. Vietnamese sources go further and credit the saponins in bồ kết with working against scalp fungus, which is a folk claim with no trial behind it and not the reason to relax about the jug. Boiling is the reason. One practical note from the same Vietnamese pages, since nobody mentions it until it happens: bồ kết water stings the eyes, so keep them shut when the second rinse goes over your forehead.

Nobody inspects the comb

It is reasonable to assume somebody does. Vietnamese business-law advisories are consistent on the shape of the rules, which is this: cutting and washing hair, industry code 9621, is not among the conditional business lines in Appendix IV of the 2020 Investment Law, so a neighbourhood shop needs household business registration and premises that meet local environmental hygiene rules. Decree 109 of 2016, the instrument that issues practice certificates to individuals and operating licences to establishments, governs medical examination and treatment, and an ordinary hair shop is not that. Those are law-firm guides rather than the statute itself, and they agree with each other. They also agree that adding massage or cosmetic procedures can move a house onto the licensed side of the line, precisely the line a gội đầu dưỡng sinh straddles, since half of what you pay for is bấm huyệt pressure work.

Nothing in any of it names a brush. So when a Vietnamese traditional-medicine doctor tells customers to check that a house is licensed, the advice quoted in the sensitive-scalp piece, he is pointing at a real document, worth having but silent on the trolley. The inspection regime you were hoping for does not exist for this trade in any country I know of, and certainly not here.

What replaces it is thirty seconds of looking, and three things are worth looking at. Whether the comb and brush go somewhere after the guest before you rather than straight back onto the trolley. Whether there is a bin beside the bed at all, since a house with no visible waste stream has no visible routine either. And whether the technician washes her hands in front of you, which in a trade where the same pair of hands works the basin and the till is not a small question. If the house also does nails, go and stand at the manicure table for a moment on your way in. Nail implements break skin, Vietnamese nail salons therefore have some sterilising routine, and that routine is on display in a way the laundry never is.

There are two things this does not depend on. Price is the first. The short wash runs 120K to 350K across the four houses in this city that publish one and the 70-to-90-minute rituals 450K to 900K across five, and the brush is the same brush; the extra money buys minutes, steam and a face, as the price table makes plain. Neighbourhood is the second, up to a point: the beach cluster of Mỹ An and An Thượng gets you a counter that can answer in English, while Hải Châu gets you a cheaper hour and more pointing, which the neighbourhood guide weighs properly. If you want the version with the fewest shared contact surfaces, it is not the Korean-style studio. Those rooms run more equipment, more product and a camera probe pressed against a parting all day; the comparison page explains what each visit is for.

Proportion, and what goes in the bag

Now the part that should calm anyone who has read this far and gone off the idea. You arrived in a city where most visitors rent a scooter within a day, and whatever helmet came clipped to the handlebar has a foam liner other heads have sweated into, worn four to six hours on a long riding day in 30-degree heat. That is the fomite exposure of your week, and the helmet piece covers what it does to your scalp before anything is caught from anywhere. One hour in a basin, where the main liquid was boiled and the water runs away, is by far the smaller number.

Lice, the fear people name when they finally say it out loud, have a clear answer. Public health agencies describe head-to-head contact as the usual route and transfer by hats, scarves, combs, brushes or towels as uncommon, and they say lice do not survive long away from a human head without giving a figure, so nobody should invent one. In a head spa the practical consequence is mild: you are lying down alone, not pressing your head against another guest's.

Some scalps should stay out of the basin this week however clean the room is, and the list is the same one Vietnamese houses publish themselves: open or broken skin, an active scalp infection or fungus, a fever. If what sent you to a search engine is a round scaly patch, an itch in one spot, or hairs that snap off short instead of falling out whole, that calls for a dermatologist rather than a technician. Da Nang has a public dermatology hospital as well as private clinics that see foreign patients; the hair-loss piece covers where those are.

Everyone else needs a bag with three things in it: a brush, a hair tie, and a case for your glasses. Then the one shift in behaviour this whole article is for. Do not ask whether the room is clean, because the answer is yes in every room on earth and it is free to give. Ask for a clean comb instead, in one short sentence, while you are still standing at the counter, and watch what happens next.

Frequently asked

Is a Vietnamese head spa hygienic?

Mostly, and the honest version is more specific than a yes. Very little in the room gets reused: the water runs once, the herbal decoction is poured from a thermos and the pot it came from simmered fifteen to thirty minutes. Reuse applies to the towel, the basin rim, the headrest and the tools, and among those the brush is the one with evidence attached. A commentary by R. J. Hay in the Journal of the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology, published in January 2026, cites a Copenhagen study in which 27 percent of brushes and 4 percent of clippers were positive for pathogenic dermatophytes, and notes that salon tools are often cleaned intermittently rather than after every customer. Ask for a clean comb, or bring your own brush.

Can you catch ringworm or a fungal infection from a hair salon?

It happens, and the published cases are almost all barbershop cases in men. Addari and colleagues reported ten male patients in Sardinia with tinea capitis after barber visits in the Journal of Clinical Medicine in 2025, with a recent barber shave as the condition for inclusion and Trichophyton tonsurans grown in all three cultures run; two of the ten were left with scarring alopecia. The comparable series in that paper include a German cohort of 18, all male with a barbershop history in every case, and a Spanish outbreak of 107 patients, 99.1 percent male. The shared exposure is a blade or clipper over freshly abraded skin at the nape, which is a haircut rather than a wash. A head spa does not cut, clip or shave you.

Can you get head lice from a head spa?

It is unlikely. Public health agencies describe head-to-head contact as the usual way lice spread and call transfer through hats, scarves, combs, brushes or towels uncommon, and they add that lice do not survive long off a human head, without publishing a survival figure. At a head spa you lie alone on a bed with your own head on a headrest, so the main route does not apply. The larger shared-item exposure of a Da Nang trip is the rental helmet you wear for hours a day, not the hour in the basin.

Should I bring my own comb or brush to a head spa in Da Nang?

Yes, and it is the cheapest useful thing in this article. The brush is the tool that drags over skin, holds other people's hair and is rinsed least, and it is the only item in a salon with a published contamination rate behind it: 27 percent of brushes in the Copenhagen study relayed by Hay in 2026. A towel tells you far less, because the finding that matters there is a wash temperature you cannot see. Akhoundi and colleagues, in the Journal of Fungi in 2022, found that laundering at 60 degrees removed dermatophyte spores from linen while heat drying and freezing did not. If you would rather not carry a brush, say cho mình xin lược mới nhé, which asks for a clean comb, at the counter rather than on the bed.
